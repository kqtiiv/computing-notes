Lemma 2 (Summands of Converging Series) | Suppose that the series of real numbers $\sum_{i=1}^\infty a_i$ converges. Then the sequence of summands has limit 0: $\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = 0$. (Note: The converse is not necessarily true! The harmonic series summands approach 0, but the series diverges).
Geometric Series | The geometric series $\sum_{n=1}^\infty x^n$ converges if $|x| < 1$, with limit $\frac{x}{1-x}$, and diverges if $|x| \ge 1$.
Harmonic Series & Inverse Squares | 1. The Harmonic Series $\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{1}{n}$ diverges to $\infty$. <br>2. The Series of Inverse Squares $\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{1}{n^2}$ converges (specifically to $\frac{\pi^2}{6}$).
Lemma 3 (Comparison Test for Series) | Let $\lambda > 0$ and $N \in \mathbb{N}$. Let $\sum c_i$ be a converging series and $\sum d_i$ a diverging series (both with positive terms). <br><br>1. If $a_i \le \lambda c_i$ for all $i > N$, then $\sum a_i$ converges.<br>2. If $a_i \ge \lambda d_i$ for all $i > N$, then $\sum a_i$ diverges.
Lemma 4 (Limit Comparison Test for Series) | Let $\sum c_i$ be a converging series and $\sum d_i$ a diverging series (positive terms).<br><br>1. If $\lim_{i \to \infty} \frac{a_i}{c_i} \in \mathbb{R}$ exists, then $\sum a_i$ converges.<br>2. If $\lim_{i \to \infty} \frac{d_i}{a_i} \in \mathbb{R}$ exists, then $\sum a_i$ diverges.
Lemma 6 (D'Alembert Limit Ratio Test) & Example | Suppose $\lim_{n \to \infty} |\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}|$ exists and equals $L$.<br>1. If $L < 1$, the series converges absolutely.<br>2. If $L > 1$, the series diverges.<br>3. If $L = 1$, the test is inconclusive.<br><br>**Example**: $\sum \frac{n^2}{2^n}$. <br>$\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{(n+1)^2/2^{n+1}}{n^2/2^n} = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{2} (\frac{n+1}{n})^2 = \frac{1}{2}$. Since $\frac{1}{2} < 1$, the series converges.
Lemma 7 (Integral Test) & Example | Let $f: [1, \infty) \to \mathbb{R}^+$ be continuous, decreasing, and positive. Let $a_n = f(n)$.<br><br>1. If $\int_{1}^\infty f(x) dx$ converges, then $\sum a_n$ converges.<br>2. If $\int_{1}^\infty f(x) dx$ diverges, then $\sum a_n$ diverges.<br><br>**Example**: $\sum \frac{1}{n^2}$. <br>$\int_1^\infty x^{-2} dx = \lim_{b \to \infty} [-x^{-1}]_1^b = 1$. The integral evaluates to a finite number, so the series converges.
Proposition 31 (The $n$-th Root Test) & Example | Consider the series $\sum a_n$. Let $L = \limsup_{n \to \infty} |a_n|^{1/n}$.<br><br>1. If $L < 1$, the series converges absolutely.<br>2. If $L > 1$, the series diverges.<br>3. If $L = 1$, the test is inconclusive.<br><br>**Example**: $\sum (\frac{n}{2n+1})^n$. <br>$L = \limsup_{n \to \infty} |(\frac{n}{2n+1})^n|^{1/n} = \limsup_{n \to \infty} \frac{n}{2n+1} = \frac{1}{2}$. Since $\frac{1}{2} < 1$, the series converges absolutely.
Definition 26 (Limit Inferior / $\liminf$) | Let $(a_n)_{n \ge 1}$ be a sequence. The limit inferior is the limit of the non-decreasing sequence $c_n = \inf \{ a_m \mid m \ge n \}$. It is denoted as $\liminf_{n \to \infty} a_n$.
Absolute vs Unconditional Convergence | A series $\sum a_i$ is **absolutely convergent** if the series of absolute values $\sum |a_i|$ converges. <br><br>If a series is absolutely convergent, it is also **unconditionally convergent**, meaning any permutation (rearrangement) of the summands will still converge to the exact same limit.
De Morgan's Laws (Sets) | For sets $S$ and $T$: $\overline{S \cup T} = \overline{S} \cap \overline{T}$ and $\overline{S \cap T} = \overline{S} \cup \overline{T}$. (The complement of a union is the intersection of the complements, and vice versa).
Injective and Surjective Functions | A function $f: S \to T$ is **onto (surjective)** if every element in $T$ has a preimage in $S$ ($f(S)=T$). It is **one-to-one (injective)** if each $b \in T$ has a *unique* preimage.
Countable Sets | An infinite set is countable if its elements can be put in a one-to-one correspondence with the set of natural numbers $\mathbb{N}$. <br><br>**Example**: The set of all integers $\mathbb{Z}$ and the set of all rational numbers $\mathbb{Q}$ are countable.
Convergence to Infinity | A sequence $(a_n)_{n \ge 1}$ converges to $\infty$ iff for all $r \in \mathbb{R}$, there is an $N \in \mathbb{N}$ such that for all $n > N$, we have $a_n > r$.
Arithmetic Sequence & Sum | A sequence where $a_n = a_1 + (n-1)d$ (with common difference $d$). <br>The sum of the first $n$ terms is: $S_n = \frac{n}{2}(a_1 + a_n)$.
Geometric Sequence & Sum | A sequence where $a_n = a r^{n-1}$ (with common ratio $r$). <br>The sum of the first $n$ terms is: $S_n = \frac{a(1-r^n)}{1-r}$ (for $r \ne 1$).
Abs 4 (Reverse Triangle Inequality) | For any real numbers $x$ and $y$, we have: $|x - y| \ge ||x| - |y||$.
Theorem 17 (Fundamental Theorem of Analysis) | A sequence of real numbers $(a_n)_{n \ge 1}$ that is **increasing** and **bounded above** converges to a limit. (That limit is the supremum of the sequence).
Theorem 14 & Proposition 15 (Subsequences) | 1. Any subsequence of a convergent sequence converges to the exact same limit as the parent sequence. <br>2. **Every** sequence of real numbers has a monotonic subsequence.
Theorem 22 (Extreme Value Theorem) | If $f: [a,b] \to \mathbb{R}$ is a continuous function on a closed, bounded interval, then $f$ attains its supremum (maximum) and infimum (minimum) in $[a,b]$.
Proposition 35 (L'Hôpital's Rule) | Suppose $f, g: (a,b) \to \mathbb{R}$ have continuous derivatives. If $f(c) = g(c) = 0$ for some $c \in (a,b)$ and $g'(c) \ne 0$, then: <br>$\lim_{x \to c} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{f'(c)}{g'(c)} = \lim_{x \to c} \frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}$.
Theorem 36 (Fundamental Theorem of Calculus) | If $f: [a,b] \to \mathbb{R}$ is continuous, and the function $F: [a,b] \to \mathbb{R}$ is defined by $F(y) = \int_a^y f(x)dx$, then $F$ is uniformly continuous on $[a,b]$ and $F'(x) = f(x)$ for $x \in (a,b)$.
Corollary 2 (Change of Variable in Integration) | Let $y = g(x)$. If $g$ is differentiable with a continuous derivative $g'$ and $f$ is continuous, then: <br>$\int_a^b f(g(x))g'(x)dx = \int_{g(a)}^{g(b)} f(y)dy$.
Definition 30 (Smoothness of a Function) | A function $f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ is considered **smooth** at $x_0$ if for all $k \ge 1$, the $k$-th derivative of $f$ exists at $x_0$.
Maclaurin Series Formula | If a function $f$ is infinitely differentiable at $x=0$, its Maclaurin series expansion is given by: <br>$f(x) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{f^{(n)}(0)}{n!} x^n$.
Newton's Method | A numerical method used to find successively better approximations to the roots (or zeroes) of a real-valued function. The sequence is defined by: <br>$x_{n+1} = x_n - \frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}$.
Standard Metrics on $\mathbb{R}^n$ | 1. **Euclidean Metric**: $d_2(x,y) = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i-y_i)^2}$ <br>2. **Taxicab Metric**: $d_1(x,y) = \sum_{i=1}^n |x_i - y_i|$ <br>3. **Maximum Metric**: $d_\infty(x,y) = \max_{1 \le i \le n} |x_i - y_i|$
